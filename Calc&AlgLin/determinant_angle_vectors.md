# Determinants, Angles, and Vectors

## 1. Determinant and the angle between two vectors

Consider two vectors in 2D:

\[
\mathbf{u} =
\begin{pmatrix}
u_x\\
u_y
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{v} =
\begin{pmatrix}
v_x\\
v_y
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Place the vectors as the columns of a matrix:

\[
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
u_x & v_x\\
u_y & v_y
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The determinant is

\[
\det(A) = u_xv_y-u_yv_x.
\]

### Geometric interpretation

The absolute value of the determinant represents the **area of the parallelogram** generated by the two vectors.

The same area can be calculated from the lengths of the vectors and the angle \(\theta\) between them:

\[
\boxed{
|\det(A)|
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\||\sin\theta|
}
\]

Therefore, the determinant is directly related to the sine of the angle:

\[
\boxed{
|\sin\theta|
=
\frac{|\det(A)|}
{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}
}
\]

### What happens for different angles?

- **\(\theta=0^\circ\)**: the vectors point in the same direction. They are parallel, so the parallelogram has zero area:

  \[
  \det(A)=0.
  \]

- **\(\theta=90^\circ\)**: the vectors are perpendicular. The parallelogram has its maximum possible area for those vector lengths:

  \[
  |\det(A)|=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|.
  \]

- **\(\theta=180^\circ\)**: the vectors point in opposite directions. They are again parallel, so:

  \[
  \det(A)=0.
  \]

### Sign of the determinant

The determinant also contains information about **orientation**.

For example:

\[
\det(A)>0
\]

means that rotating \(\mathbf{u}\) toward \(\mathbf{v}\) follows the counterclockwise orientation.

Conversely,

\[
\det(A)<0
\]

indicates the clockwise orientation.

Thus, the determinant provides both:

1. The magnitude of the area spanned by the vectors.
2. The orientation of that area.

### Important connection with linear dependence

If

\[
\det(A)=0,
\]

the vectors do not span any area. This happens when they are linearly dependent, meaning that one vector is a scalar multiple of the other:

\[
\mathbf{v}=c\mathbf{u}.
\]

So the determinant can also be used to detect whether two 2D vectors are linearly independent.

## 2. Dot product and the angle between two vectors

The dot product provides another way of relating two vectors to their angle.

For

\[
\mathbf{u} =
\begin{pmatrix}
u_x\\
u_y
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{v} =
\begin{pmatrix}
v_x\\
v_y
\end{pmatrix},
\]

the dot product is

\[
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
=
u_xv_x+u_yv_y.
\]

Geometrically, it satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta
}
\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors.

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{
\cos\theta
=
\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}
{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}
}
\]

### What does the dot product tell us?

The sign and magnitude of the dot product describe the relationship between the directions of the vectors.

- **\(\theta=0^\circ\)**:

  \[
  \cos(0^\circ)=1
  \]

  so

  \[
  \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
  =
  \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|.
  \]

  The vectors point in the same direction.

- **\(\theta=90^\circ\)**:

  \[
  \cos(90^\circ)=0
  \]

  so

  \[
  \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0.
  \]

  The vectors are perpendicular.

- **\(\theta=180^\circ\)**:

  \[
  \cos(180^\circ)=-1
  \]

  so

  \[
  \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
  =
  -\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|.
  \]

  The vectors point in opposite directions.

### Determinant versus dot product

The determinant and dot product provide complementary information about the angle.

The dot product is related to the **cosine**:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta
}
\]

while the determinant is related to the **sine**:

\[
\boxed{
|\det(A)|
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\||\sin\theta|
}
\]

This gives the following comparison:

| Quantity | Geometric interpretation | Angular relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Dot product | Alignment / projection | \(\cos\theta\) |
| Determinant | Area / separation | \(\sin\theta\) |

Consequently, the angle can be recovered from the dot product and determinant (in 2D) using:

\[
\boxed{
\theta
=
\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
\det(A),
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
\right)
}
\]

The `atan2` formulation is particularly useful because it preserves the orientation given by the sign of the determinant.

---

## Summary

For two vectors, the dot product and determinant describe complementary aspects of their relative orientation:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\det(A)
=
\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta
}
\]

The **dot product measures how much the vectors point in the same direction**, while the **determinant measures how much they spread apart and the orientation of that spreading**.
